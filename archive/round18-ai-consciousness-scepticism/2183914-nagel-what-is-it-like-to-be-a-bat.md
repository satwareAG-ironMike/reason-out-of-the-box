## Summary

The anchor for all three sceptical arguments. Nagel defines consciousness by the
subjective character of experience and argues that objective physical accounts leave
the essential fact out. The reprinted *Minds and Nature* volume supplies the further
claim the anchor cites: mental processes are not psychological processes, so the
materialist hypothesis is not well formulated.

## Abstract

No formal abstract; the essay opens: "Consciousness is what makes the mind-body
problem really intractable. Perhaps the difficulty is a result of the closeness of
the connections between consciousness, intentionality, and the physical."

## Key findings

Quoted from the printed text (pages 435-450):

- "an organism has conscious mental states if and only if there is something that it
  is like to be that organism - something it is like for the organism"
- "We may call this the subjective character of experience", and it "is not
  confined to perception: the subjective character of experience is fully
  comprehensive throughout the mental. If it were not the phenomenon under discussion
  it would be impossible to explain what the problem is, and impossible to explain why
  no current solution to it is acceptable."
- The bat argument: echolocation is "a form of perception - at once so remote, so
  primitive, and so special, that the difference in subjective character from what
  acoustic and visual perception entails for humans exceeds that between whatever any
  human can be and the kind of thing he is." Bats have subjective character "though
  they may possess fewer of the faculties that humans have, and it is probably unlike
  to be as rich and various."
- The limits of extrapolation: "the fact that an organism's overt behaviour does not
  suggest conscious experience is not sufficient evidence that the organism is not
  conscious either", and "the extrapolation must be incompletable. We cannot form more
  than a schematic conception of what it is like to be a bat or an eagle or a
  dolphin or a bat or an ant, or a dragonfly."
- Against reductive programmes: "It is useless to base the defence of materialism on
  any analysis of mental phenomena that fails to deal explicitly with their subjective
  character. For it is this feature, and this alone, that gives rise to the mind-body
  problem in the first place." Nagel also rejects dualism; he proposes "a wider
  physicalist view, teleological in form" as the best available speculation, and
  concludes "physicalism is a position we cannot understand because we do not at
  present have any conception of how it might be true".
- The bat sonar passage (page 440): "the sonar appears to make a system related to
  the environment and not to the brain, because it generates an objective view of a
  world where no subjective point of view exists", an example of "how easily the
  subject can be projected into the object, and the objectivity of the world
  mistaken for the objectivity of the physical".
- In *Minds and Nature* (chapter 9, "What is it like to be a bat?"): "mental processes
  are not psychological processes. That is the difference between this hypothesis and
  psycho-physical materialism proper, which maintains that mental processes -
  consciousness, intentionality, and the rest - just are psychological processes, and
  that the mind is part of the physical world in the same way that other familiar
  objects and events are." Nagel's point about Batson's echo-location experiment is
  that "what Batson has discovered, assuming that his interpretation of his results is
  correct, is not something that Batson is conscious of perceiving but something which
  his sonar system perceives."

## Method

Essay with thought experiments (the bat, a Martians' bat, Batson's
echo-location experiment) and a discussion of Nagel's earlier self ("The
absence of a conception of the relation between subjective and objective is what
makes the problem of mind and body intractable, and this is the point of the bat
example").

## Relevance to the core question

The anchor's first reason ("AI could lack subjective experience") is Nagel's
position. His own framing of the limits of extrapolation is double-edged: absence of
behavioural evidence is not evidence of absence, so his argument does not show that
current AI systems are not conscious. In *Minds and Nature* he adds the
"objective phenomenology" programme - "an objective phenomenology not dependent on
empathy or the imagination, and not subject to the usual constraints of too little
information to go on" - as the only way to approach other- minds.

## Limitations and counterarguments

Consciousness-as-subjectivity cannot be measured from outside; the argument gives no
criterion for a machine. It is compatible with AI consciousness in principle and
incompatible with knowing about it, which is why the anchor treats it as a reason for
uncertainty rather than for capability limits.
