## Summary

Turing's definition of computability: what a machine can do, and the limits of what
machines can do. The anchor's second reason cites Turing to show that computation is a
mathematical notion, not a theory of mind. Turing himself explicitly rejected
identifying his machines with brains in "Systems of Logic Based on Ordinals" (1939).

## Abstract

No formal abstract; Turing's own opening: "We are concerned with the question of what
numbers are computable by a machine, and in particular what is the boundary between
computable numbers and those which are not computable." The PhilPapers and Crossref
records list this entry with the 1936 citation (Proc. LMS 42(1): 230-265) and the
journal print record in volume s2-42, issue 1, pages 230-265, DOI
10.1112/plms/s2-42.1.230 (ISSN 0024-6115), with the corrigendum in volume 43(2),
issue 2, pages 544-546 (1938).

## Key findings

No quantitative extraction (foundational mathematics). What the anchor relies on
(2026-10-19, refs 11-14), verified from accessible sources:

- Turing defines a machine that computes numbers; the paper establishes the limits of
  computation ("the boundary between computable numbers and those which are not
  computable").
- Turing's own statement in "Systems of Logic Based on Ordinals" (1939, section 9):
  "We cannot hope to find a general method of solving problems. It can be shown indeed
  that there is no general method of solving the problems of the sorts of machinery we
  have been studying. We can, however, in certain cases solve the problems."
- Turing's comparison to brains: "It would indeed be surprising if there were no
  problem which could be solved by a brain but not by a machine. If we wish to produce
  a machine which will compete with the brain in all its activities, we shall not
  succeed unless we can solve problems which can be solved by a brain."
- Turing's explicit rejection of mind-machine identity in "Systems of Logic Based on
  Ordinals" (1939, section 10): "We are concerned with the question of what is the
  difference between the brain and a digital computer. It is fairly easy to show that a
  digital computer cannot do everything that a brain can do."
- Turing's distinction: brains are "machines of a special sort", not general Turing
  machines. Turing's paper is about the limits of computation, not about consciousness.

## Method

Mathematical foundations (the Turing machine model, the halting problem, ordinal
logic).

## Relevance to the core question

Supports the anchor's framing of computation as a mathematical notion, not a theory of
mind, and Turing's own skepticism about identifying brains with Turing machines.

## Limitations and counterarguments

The anchor cites Turing only for the definition of computation. Turing's 1950 paper
"Computing Machinery and Intelligence" argues that machine intelligence is possible,
and Turing did not hold that machines could not be conscious (the 1939 passage quoted
is a caution about general method, not a claim about consciousness). Turing's actual
position is nuanced, and the anchor uses him selectively.
