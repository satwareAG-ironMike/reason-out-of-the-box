## Summary

Searle's statement of biological naturalism: consciousness is a biological feature of
certain brains, and no computational description of a system is sufficient for
consciousness. The anchor's third reason is built on this claim, and Seth's 2025
target article develops the same position in detail.

## Abstract

No formal abstract; the chapter's argument is summarized in the anchor and in Seth's
target article. The Blackwell Companion's catalog record confirms the chapter title
and the volume and page range.

## Key findings

No quantitative extraction (book chapter; abstract only). The claims used in this round:

- Searle's formula (quoted via Seth 2025, verified from accessible text): "mental
  states are caused by and realized by lower-level neurobiological processes ...
  mental states are biological features at higher levels"
- Searle's argument against computational accounts of consciousness: "the brain's
  causal powers go all the way down to the molecular level and no further, and at the
  molecular level, the specific biological nature of the causal powers is what
  matters, not a description of information flows." (quoted via Seth's summary of
  Searle's argument)
- The Turing Test argument: Searle's view that no program running on any hardware can
  produce consciousness purely from its computational organization
- In this Companion volume, the chapter covers the biological naturalism thesis as a
  response to functionalist accounts of mind.

## Method

Argumentative chapter in a handbook volume.

## Relevance to core question

The source of the anchor's third reason. Searle's position is the strongest version of
the "biology is special" claim, and Seth's target article (see the Seth 2025 entry) is
the version the anchor actually uses. Seth's argument, not Searle's, is the one the
anchor cites in detail.

## Limitations and counterarguments

Searle's claim is asserted, not demonstrated: no experiment shows that a functional
organization realized in silicon lacks experience. Block's reply (see the Block 2026
entry) attacks the move from "biological causes matter" to "only biological systems can
be conscious". The anchor's own wording, "we do not know whether biology is special",
is more modest than Searle's.
