## Abstract (condensed)

Tests whether humans use the same strategies as other species for transitive inference (TI), a
problem that non-humans can solve by associative learning rather than logic. Cross-species
generalizations about neural function may be invalid: humans may use different strategies mediated
by alternative structures. Results bear on the assumption of similar neural substrates in humans and
other animals.

## Key findings (verified from PubMed abstract; venue/DOI verified from the PubMed record)

- Successful TI in humans is unrelated to the use of associative-learning strategies.
- Successful human TI is associated with the ability to report the hierarchical relationship among
  stimuli.
- Concludes cross-species generalizations must be interpreted cautiously: the same task may be
  mediated by different strategies and/or neural systems across species.

## Relevance to core question

This human TI baseline splits the field's dispute in the direction that matters for the thesis
(issue #74 required human baselines): in humans, passing the test tracks an articulable hierarchy,
not the associative route that explains many animal results (contrast Frank 2005 and Vasconcelos 2008
this round). It is therefore the comparison point for the human arm of the study design (issues #7,
#13): a pass that correlates with an explicit relational report is a different kind of evidence from
a pass that correlates with reinforcement history. No acceptance criterion is met (human subjects,
not an LLM), so the entry is the human-comparison design and the caution against reading a shared
behavior as a shared mechanism. Verdict: mixed - the analogy is not automatic in either direction.
