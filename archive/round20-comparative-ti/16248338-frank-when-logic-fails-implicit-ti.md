## Abstract (condensed)

Transitive inference (TI) in animals (choosing A over C from A>B and B>C) has been read by some as a
declarative logical process. The authors invert that anthropomorphic reading and test whether humans
show TI-like behavior from simpler associative mechanisms. Humans trained on a five-pair TI problem
(A+B-, B+C-, C+D-, D+E-, E+F-) were prevented from becoming explicitly aware of the hierarchy, so
they could not use logical reasoning, then tested on B vs D, B vs E, and C vs E.

## Key findings (verified from PubMed abstract; venue/DOI verified from the PubMed record)

- Participants reliably chose B over E only; the other test conditions were at chance.
- That pattern is inconsistent with logical reasoning and matches an associative account built for
  earlier rat TI studies that found the same pattern (differential associative strengths across
  items developed over training despite equal overt reinforcement).
- The result is a human demonstration of the associative shortcut the animal literature disputes.

## Relevance to core question

This is a human TI baseline and, more sharply, the paired-associate critique in human form (issue
#74 required both): it shows that a species known to reason logically still produces a TI pass from
associative strength alone when explicit knowledge is withheld. That is the exact confound this
study must exclude on its own ladder scores and on any animal analogy in the thesis's opening
question. It opposes the strong reading of the analogy: a transitive-closure pass does not
demonstrate inference, even in humans, so the study's Condition 3 (process-level causal evidence) is
not optional. No acceptance criterion is met (human subjects, not an LLM), so the entry is the
control design and the caution, not the machine evidence.
