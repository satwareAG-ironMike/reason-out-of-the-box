## Abstract (condensed)

Rhesus macaques and humans performed a transitive-inference (TI) task in which, before learning a
7-item list (ABCDEFG), a block of trials presented only the pair FG. The high prior rate of reward
for F was expected by associative models to disrupt later learning of the whole list.

## Key findings (verified from PubMed abstract; venue/DOI verified from the PubMed record)

- The high prior reward for F did not disrupt subsequent list learning, contrary to associative
  models' expectation.
- Monkeys, who each completed many sessions with novel stimuli, learned to anticipate that novel
  stimuli should be preferred over F; the authors read this as a task representation of TI that
  generalizes beyond specific stimuli.
- Task-naive humans showed a transitory bias to F with novel stimuli but rapidly unlearned it.
- The authors conclude the results are hard to reconcile with any account that assigns strength to
  individual stimulus-reward associations, and support sophisticated cognitive processes in both
  species, with species differences.

## Relevance to core question

This is the counterweight in the comparative-TI dispute and the reason the exclusion at INDEX line
458 cannot be stated flatly (issue #74): where Frank 2005 and Vasconcelos 2008 argue the associative
reading, this study finds a TI pattern that associative strength models do not predict and that
generalizes to novel stimuli in monkeys. It is the methodological model for a control that could
decide the question - perturb the reward structure and see whether the transitive pattern survives.
The thesis needs exactly this style of control for its ladder family and #71's depth ceiling: a pass
that survives an associative-disruptive manipulation is evidence, a pass that tracks reward history
is not. No acceptance criterion is met (animal and human subjects), so the entry is the control
paradigm and the evidence that the analogy's mechanism is genuinely open. Verdict: mixed.
