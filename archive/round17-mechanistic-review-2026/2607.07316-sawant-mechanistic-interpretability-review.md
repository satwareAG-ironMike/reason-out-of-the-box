## Abstract (condensed)
Scoping review of mechanistic interpretability (MI), the reverse-engineering of the
internal algorithms of modern neural networks. Three pillars: Transformer circuit
analysis (residual stream, QK/OV attention circuits, induction heads, universality
hypothesis, IOI circuits, Automated Circuit Discovery, edge attribution patching),
decomposition of polysemantic representations (superposition, Sparse Autoencoders,
feature transcoders, cross-layer transcoders), and steering vectors plus neurosymbolic
(NeSy) rule extraction that translates neural representations into executable logical
rules.

## Key findings (verified from full text)
- Section 3.8 "Emergent Symbolic Reasoning Mechanisms" (focus of this ingest):
  circuit-level mechanisms as candidates for abstract, multi-step reasoning rather
  than mere pattern completion.
- Brinkmann et al. (arXiv:2402.11917, 2024): mechanistic analysis of a Transformer
  trained on a synthetic symbolic multi-step task (path-finding within a tree).
  Linear probing plus causal interventions identified "deduction heads", described as
  "a specialized variant of induction heads that iteratively move information one
  level up the tree across multiple consecutive layers", enabling several tree edges
  to be traversed within one forward pass. Also a "parallelization motif" in which
  early layers "simultaneously resolve several sub-paths that may later prove
  relevant" instead of committing to one sequential search.
- Yang et al. (ICML 2025, "Emergent symbolic mechanisms support abstract reasoning
  in large language models"): a three-stage symbolic architecture in LLMs. (i) Early
  layers: symbol abstraction heads "convert concrete input tokens into abstract
  variables based on the relations between them". (ii) Intermediate layers: symbolic
  induction heads "perform sequence induction directly over these abstract variables
  rather than over surface tokens". (iii) Later layers: retrieval heads "predict the
  next token by retrieving the value bound to the predicted abstract variable". The
  review frames this as mechanistic evidence of learned variable binding and symbol
  manipulation that reconciles the symbolic-versus-neural debate, and notes that "a
  degree of symbolic structure can emerge inside Transformers even before any
  explicit symbolic component is added".
- Surrounding scope of the review: induction heads form as a sudden phase transition
  (clamping study: three sub-circuits - PT-attend & copy, IH QK match, output copy -
  plus massive head redundancy); the full IOI circuit of 26 attention heads in
  GPT-2 small (Duplicate Token, S-Inhibition, Name Mover heads); ACDC's backward
  iterative ablation over a DAG of components; SAE monosemantic features and
  cross-layer transcoders; the universality hypothesis as the premise that makes
  circuit transfer across models plausible.

## Relevance to core question
**Supports the mechanism line: symbolic structure can form inside trained
transformers.** Section 3.8 is the strongest mechanistic bridge between symbolic and
neural accounts of reasoning in this archive: variable binding, symbolic induction,
and deduction-like circuits are identified with causal methods. Against the core
question it stops short on every strict condition: this is a survey (preprint, no
experiments of its own); Brinkmann et al.'s model is trained on the synthetic tree
task (task-trained, not out of the box); the LLMs in Yang et al.'s ICML 2025 study
are not identified here, so training-data disclosure cannot be established. Meets
open-problem condition 3 (process-level circuit evidence) only for a task-trained
model; conditions 1, 2, and 5 (base model, post-cutoff procedural novelty,
depth generalization) are not met. Verdict: +/- mechanism - strengthens the
Nanda/Olsson line (real circuits form spontaneously or via task training) without
supporting the out-of-the-box claim.
