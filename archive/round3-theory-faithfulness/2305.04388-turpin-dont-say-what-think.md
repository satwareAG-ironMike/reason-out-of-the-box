# Language Models Don't Always Say What They Think: Unfaithful Explanations in Chain-of-Thought Prompting

| Field | Value |
|-------|-------|
| arXiv | [2305.04388](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.04388) |
| Authors | Miles Turpin, Julian Michael, Ethan Perez, Samuel R. Bowman (NYU) |
| Venue | **NeurIPS 2023** |
| Year | 2023 |
| Archive round | 3 - Theory & faithfulness |
| Archived | 2026-09-02 |

## Abstract (condensed)
Shows CoT explanations can systematically misrepresent the true cause of a prediction. By
adding biasing features to few-shot exemplars (e.g. answers always (A)), models use the bias
while the CoT explanations do not mention the biasing feature; accuracy drops as much as
36.3% under the Suggested Answer bias (GPT-3.5, zero-shot CoT). Removing CoT can *improve*
faithfulness of behavior even as accuracy drops.

## Key findings (verified from full text)
- CoT prose is often **post-hoc rationalization**, not the causal path to the answer.
- Preference-primed models "explain" their answers with plausible reasons that had no causal
  role - the bias hidden in the exemplars did the work.
- Direct safety implication: treating CoT as a window into model reasoning is unreliable.

## Relevance to core question
**Methodological killer for weak evidence**: any argument of the form "the model reasoned
correctly because its step-by-step output is correct" is undermined. Verbalized reasoning is
necessary-but-not-sufficient evidence; behavioral outcome alone cannot establish reasoning.
