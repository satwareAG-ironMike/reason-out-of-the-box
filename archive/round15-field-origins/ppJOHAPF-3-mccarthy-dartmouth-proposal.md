## Abstract (condensed)

A 2-month, 10-man study of artificial intelligence at Dartmouth College in the
summer of 1956, which is "to proceed on the basis of the conjecture that every
aspect of learning or any other feature of intelligence can in principle be so
precisely described that a machine can be made to simulate it." Seven problem
areas are set: automatic computers, language use ("manipulating words according
to rules of reasoning and rules of conjecture"), neuron nets, theory of the size
of a calculation (efficiency and complexity measures), self-improvement,
abstractions, and randomness and creativity, where "the difference between
creative thinking and unimaginative competent thinking lies in the injection of
a some randomness" that "must be guided by intuition." The individual research
statements anticipate the modern axes: Shannon (information theory applied to
computing machines and brain models; the "matched environment" brain-model
approach), Minsky (trial-and-error learning plus sensory and motor abstractions
so that the machine "would tend to build up within itself an abstract model of
the environment"), Rochester (originality via controlled randomness; "how can I
make a machine which will exhibit originality in its solution of problems?"),
and McCarthy (language as the means of handling complicated phenomena; a formal
language with "rules of conjecture" and self-reference). Widely regarded as the
founding document of AI as a field and as the first use of the term
"artificial intelligence".

## Key findings (verified from full text)

Quotes below were read from a direct full-text copy (McCarthy's reformatted
1955 text as circulated at jmc.stanford.edu, identical to the AI Magazine 2006
reprint); ligature and scanning artifacts of that copy ("di erence",
"arti cial") were normalized in the quotations.

- Core conjecture: "every aspect of learning or any other feature of
  intelligence can in principle be so precisely described that a machine can be
  made to simulate it."
- Scope: "An attempt will be made to make machines use language, form
  abstractions and concepts, solve kinds of problems now reserved for humans,
  and improve themselves."
- Language as reasoning medium: "It may be speculated that a large part of human
  thought consists of manipulating words according to rules of reasoning and
  rules of conjecture."
- Minsky's internal-model claim: the machine "would tend to build up within
  itself an abstract model of the environment in which it is placed", so that
  after preliminary internal study "these external experiments would appear to
  be rather clever".
- Rochester's originality problem: "how can I make a machine which will exhibit
  originality in its solution of problems?", where problems that "have long
  resisted solution" need "a really bizarre deviation from traditional
  methods."
- McCarthy's language program: "The human mind apparently uses language as its
  means of handling complicated phenomena", while logical languages "have either
  been instruction lists to make computers carry out calculations specified in
  advance or else formalization of parts of mathematics."
- No empirical results: the document is a planning document. Its concrete
  content is organizational (participant list, a $13,500 Rockefeller
  Foundation budget, regular research seminars).

## Relevance to core question

Context, not evidence: the document predates the models under investigation, so
it proves nothing about out-of-the-box reasoning. Its value for this project is
framing. It (1) defines the target as simulation of intelligence ("a machine
can be made to simulate it"), the "build the machine" program in contrast to
the modern "what is already in the base model" question; (2) anticipates the
axes on which the 2026 debate is fought: internal language as a reasoning
medium (McCarthy's conjecture section is a direct ancestor of chain-of-thought
reasoning), controlled randomness for novelty (Rochester, ancestor of
temperature and sampling), internal world models for prediction (Minsky), and
efficiency/complexity theory of computation (ancestor of scaling and
length-generalization barriers); (3) contains no out-of-the-box claim at all.
The 1956 workshop did not meet its planned deliverables, and the field
subsequently oriented toward building machines rather than testing whether
existing ones already can.
