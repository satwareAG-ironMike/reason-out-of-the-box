## Abstract (condensed)
Two propositions: intentionality is a product of the brain's causal features, and
instantiating a computer program is never by itself sufficient for intentionality. The
Chinese Room thought experiment argues the point: a monolingual English speaker follows
rule-governed symbol manipulation for Chinese and still understands nothing, so syntax
alone never yields semantics. Broader conclusion: computation as such is not the essence
of mind; brains cause minds through their specific causal powers.

## Key findings (verified from full text)
- The formal claim (proposition 2, abstract): "Instantiating a computer program is never
  by itself a sufficient condition of intentionality"; in the target article's own
  framing: "Could instantiating a program, the right program of course, by itself be a
  sufficient condition of understanding?"
- Syntax-semantics gap: "the formal symbol manipulations by themselves don't have any
  intentionality; they are quite meaningless... In the linguistic jargon, they have only
  a syntax but no semantics."
- Substrate intuition: "the same program could have all sorts of crazy realizations that
  had no form of intentionality" (water pipes, wind machines, toilet paper and stones);
  "only something that has the same causal powers as brains can have intentionality."
- Opening concession often overlooked: "Could a machine think? The answer is, obviously,
  yes. We are precisely such machines" - the argument targets program-instantiation, not
  artificial minds per se.
- The BBS open-commentary stream (part of the same package, pp 425-457) is where the
  canonical replies were born; Dennett's commentary is present in the scanned issue
  (see the round 13 Dennett entry).

## Relevance to core question
**Contra (theoretical) on machine understanding; no bearing on the reasoning question.**
This is the root of the syntax-vs-semantics line carried in round 8 by Harnad (symbol
grounding) and Lerchner (mapmaker dependence). It also contains the ancestor of the
recurring public argument "run the same math by hand on paper - would you call the paper
sentient?" The paper establishes only that one *intuition* about such hand-execution is
negative; whether that intuition transfers to every implementation of a computation is
the contested premise (see the round 13 verdict addendum). Searle proves nothing about
out-of-the-box reasoning, training data disclosure, or any acceptance criterion; the
overall verdict is unchanged.
