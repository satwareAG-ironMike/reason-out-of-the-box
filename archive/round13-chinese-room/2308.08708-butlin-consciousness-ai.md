## Abstract (condensed)
A rigorous, empirically grounded approach to AI consciousness: derive "indicator
properties" in computational terms from the best-supported neuroscientific theories
(recurrent processing theory, global workspace theory, higher-order theories, predictive
processing, attention schema theory), then assess real AI systems against them. No
claims from any single theory are treated as settled; the indicators are what the
theories agree on.

## Key findings (verified from full text)
- The headline result (abstract): "Our analysis suggests that no current AI systems are
  conscious, but also suggests that there are no obvious technical barriers to building
  AI systems which satisfy these indicators."
- The authors amended an earlier wording in a footnote: "no obvious barriers to building
  conscious AI systems" became "no obvious technical barriers to building AI systems
  which satisfy these indicators", stressing that "satisfying the indicators would not
  mean that such an AI system would definitely be conscious."
- Method: survey the theories, "derive 'indicator properties' of consciousness,
  elucidated in computational terms", then score systems against them - a graded,
  theory-anchored assessment rather than a yes/no intuition.

## Relevance to core question
**Assessment frame for the sentience debate; no bearing on the reasoning question.**
This is what the serious version of the public consciousness debate looks like once the
Chinese-Room-style intuitions are set aside: theory-derived, checkable indicator
properties and a graded evaluation of actual systems. Its double verdict (no current
system qualifies; no barrier in principle) is exactly the two-sided conclusion the
intuition-pump arguments cannot deliver. It establishes nothing about out-of-the-box
reasoning and meets none of the three acceptance criteria; the overall verdict is
unchanged.
