## Abstract

Condensed from the PubMed record. Abstract-level verification, not full-text verified.
Whether animals experience human-like emotions is controversial; because animals cannot
provide subjective reports, emotional state can only be inferred using physiological,
cognitive, and behavioral measures. In humans, negative feelings correlate reliably with
pessimistic cognitive biases (increased expectation of bad outcomes). Pessimistic-like
decision making had been found in mammals and birds with poor welfare, but cognitive biases
had not been explored in invertebrates. The paper tests whether honeybees display a
pessimistic cognitive bias under an anxiety-like state induced by vigorous shaking designed
to simulate a predatory attack.

## Key findings

- Agitated bees were more likely to classify ambiguous stimuli as predicting punishment
  (state-dependent modulation of categorization).
- Shaken bees had lower levels of dopamine, octopamine, and serotonin in their hemolymph.
- The authors conclude that the bees' response to a negatively valenced event has more in
  common with that of vertebrates than previously thought, and that the finding "suggests
  that honeybees could be regarded as exhibiting emotions" (abstract wording).

## Relevance to core question

Foundational negative-valence evidence in the bee chain: state-dependent cognitive
evaluation in an insect brain, linked to neuromodulatory signatures shared with vertebrates.
Start of the substrate-threshold argument chain (docs/claims-insect-sentience.md). Not
reasoning evidence; overall verdict unchanged.
