## Abstract

Condensed from the PubMed record. Abstract-level verification, not full-text verified.
Behavioral flexibility - adjusting behavior to changing environmental demands - is limited in
most invertebrates. The paper uses a novel task: bumblebees must move a small ball into a
designated central area to obtain a food reward; bees were pre-trained on the correct ball
location.

## Key findings

- Bees that observed the technique from a live or model demonstrator learned the task more
  efficiently than bees exposed to a ghost control (ball moved by a magnet) or no
  demonstration.
- Observers did not copy demonstrators moving balls over long distances: they solved the task
  more efficiently by using the ball positioned closest to the target, even if it was of a
  different color than the one previously observed.
- The authors call this unprecedented cognitive flexibility and state that entirely novel
  behaviors could emerge relatively swiftly in species whose lifestyle demands advanced
  learning abilities, should relevant ecological pressures arise.

## Relevance to core question

Two roles in this POC: (1) context for counterargument #9 - an animal solving a novel task
without trial-and-error training on the task itself, the animal analogue of reasoning without
task training; (2) the behavioral precursor of the play paper (entry 5): the ball-rolling
behavior later analyzed as play. Not LLM evidence; overall verdict unchanged.
