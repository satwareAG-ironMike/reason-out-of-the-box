## Abstract

Condensed from the PubMed record. Abstract-level verification, not full-text verified.
Insects are claimed not to show self-protective behavior toward noxiously stimulated body
parts, but this claim had never been empirically tested. The paper tests a type of
self-protective behavior in an insect: self-grooming a noxiously stimulated site.

## Key findings

- Bumblebees (Bombus terrestris) were touched on an antenna with a noxiously heated (65 °C)
  probe.
- In the first 2 min after the stimulus, bees groomed their touched antenna more than their
  untouched antenna, and more than bees touched with an unheated probe or not touched at all
  did.
- The authors present this as evidence that bumblebees display self-protective behavior and
  discuss the potential neural mechanisms and the implications for whether insects feel pain.

## Relevance to core question

Adds a third behavioural indicator (site-specific self-grooming) to the bee chain, testing a
claim that was previously assumed but untested. Not reasoning evidence; overall verdict
unchanged.
