## Abstract

Condensed from the PubMed record. Abstract-level verification, not full-text verified.
Modulation of nociception lets animals optimize survival by adapting behaviour to context.
In mammals this is executed by neurons from the brain: descending control of nociception.
Whether insects have such control, or the neural circuits allowing it, has rarely been
explored. Based on behavioural, neuroscientific, and molecular evidence, the paper argues
that insects probably have descending controls for nociception.

## Key findings

- Behavioural work shows insects can modulate nocifensive behaviour; such modulation is at
  least in part controlled by the central nervous system, since the information mediating
  the prioritization is processed by the brain.
- Neuroanatomical and neurobiological evidence shows the insect brain can facilitate or
  suppress nocifensive behaviour.
- Molecular studies reveal pathways involved in the inhibition of nocifensive behaviour both
  peripherally and centrally.
- Insects lack the endogenous opioid peptides and receptors that contribute to mammalian
  descending nociception controls; the authors discuss likely alternative molecular
  mechanisms.
- The paper discusses what descending control may reveal about pain perception in insects
  and the ethical implications.

## Relevance to core question

Neural plausibility layer for the trade-off paper (entry 6): top-down modulation of
nociception requires brain-level processing in an insect central nervous system. Not
reasoning evidence; overall verdict unchanged.
